About the Author

He writes what men are told not to say.

General Mamba is the author of four books of nonfiction, poetry, and fiction exploring memory, Black masculinity, and the quiet architecture of grace. His essays have appeared in The Atlantic, Harper’s Magazine, and The Paris Review.

A former high school literature teacher and recipient of a Whiting Award in Nonfiction, he divides his time between Brooklyn and the South Carolina Lowcountry.

Excerpt • Chapter One

The Weight of Grace

My father kept his Sunday shoes in a cedar box beneath the floorboards of the hall closet, wrapped in torn sheets of the Charleston News and Courier. Before he ever spoke a word from the pulpit, he spent forty minutes every Saturday night working oxblood polish into the creases with an old cotton undershirt.

“A man is judged first by what holds him up,” he told me once, not looking up from his rag. He did not mean leather or Goodyear welts. He meant posture. He meant the refusal to let anyone see the strain in your shoulders when the rent came due in November.

It took me thirty years to understand that silence is its own kind of weight—and that setting it down is not a failure of strength, but the beginning of grace.
